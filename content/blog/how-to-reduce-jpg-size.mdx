JPG remains the default format for everyday photos. If your files feel heavy, you usually do not need new software—just a clearer process.

## Why JPG files get large

Camera phones capture a lot of detail and metadata. Social apps may recompress images, but originals from your gallery can still be several megabytes each. That is fine for archives, not for a 200KB upload limit.

## Lower quality carefully

JPG compression discards some information on purpose. A quality setting around 70–85 often looks nearly identical to the original for web use, while cutting a surprising amount of weight.

Use a before/after preview. Zoom into faces, text overlays and product edges. If those still look clean, you are in a good range.

## Shrink dimensions first

If an image will be shown at 1200px wide, exporting at 3000px only increases file size. Resize to the real target, then compress. This two-step approach beats aggressive quality reduction alone.

## Remove what you do not need

Screenshots saved as JPG, busy backgrounds and unnecessary borders all cost bytes. Crop tightly. For graphics with flat colors, consider PNG or WebP instead.

## Aim for common limits

Many portals ask for 100KB or 200KB. CompressKit’s target tools iterate quality (and sometimes dimensions) to approach those limits. Expect approximate results—image content decides how far compression can go.

## Quick checklist

- Resize to final display size
- Use Balanced or High Quality presets
- Preview important details
- Fall back to target-size mode for strict upload caps
- Keep a copy of the original when the photo matters

Smaller JPGs make sites faster, forms happier and sharing less painful—without turning every photo into a blurry mess.
